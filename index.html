How it's measured
  1. Finding down. A slow 1-second average of the accelerometer follows gravity. Whatever is left over, projected onto gravity, is the vertical jolt. This is why the phone can lie any way up.
  2. Comfort. The root-mean-square of the vertical jolt over the last 5 s, matched to the ISO 2631-1 bands: < 0.315, 0.315–0.63, 0.63–1.0, 1.0–1.6, 1.6–2.5, > 2.5 m/s². Where the standard's bands overlap, this app uses the upper edge.
  3. Bumps. A jolt over the threshold starts an event. Its peak over the next 0.3 s is pinned at the latest GPS fix, then the app waits 1 s so one pothole isn't counted twice.
  4. Map colour. Between two good GPS fixes (accuracy 35 m or better, at least 3 m apart), the RMS of every jolt in between colours that stretch.
  • About 60 readings a second, so vibration up to about 30 Hz shows up. Body vibration in vehicles sits below that.
  • ISO 2631 also weights frequencies by how much people feel them. This app doesn't, so its value is an unweighted estimate.
  • GPS is good to about 5–10 m, and picking up the phone makes false bumps.

How it's measured
  1. Three directions. A slow 3-second average finds gravity, which gives up–down. It nearly freezes during a push, so braking isn't read as a tilt. The phone's long edge, or the back of the phone in a holder, projected flat gives forward–back. Sideways is at right angles to both.
  2. Vibration against pushes. Movement slower than about 0.3 s (braking, pulling away, cornering) is split off as a push. What's left is vibration.
  3. Comfort. ISO 2631-1 total value av = √(1.4²·ax² + 1.4²·ay² + az²), from 5 s RMS values. Horizontal vibration counts 1.4× because seated people feel it more. Bands: < 0.315, 0.315–0.63, 0.63–1.0, 1.0–1.6, 1.6–2.5, > 2.5 m/s².
  4. Harsh moves. A push over 2.5 m/s² (about 0.25 g) for at least 0.3 s. It's a turn if it's mostly sideways. Otherwise GPS speed before and after tells a brake from a start.
  5. Trip score. The whole-trip av, the share of time in the two comfortable bands, and harsh moves per 10 minutes.
  • The ISO frequency weightings (Wk, Wd) aren't applied, so values are unweighted estimates. Compare trips with each other rather than with lab figures.
  • About 60 readings a second limits vibration to about 30 Hz. Vehicle ride vibration sits mostly at 1–20 Hz.
  • Holding the phone in your hand adds your own movement. Rest it on a seat, your lap or a holder.

How it's measured
  1. Directions. Gravity gives up–down. The phone's long edge, or the back of the phone in a holder, laid flat gives forward–back. Sideways is at right angles to both.
  2. Harsh moves. Slow pushes (smoothed over 0.3 s) over 2.5 m/s² for at least 0.3 s. Mostly sideways means a sharp turn. For forward–back, the app learns which way is forward by matching pushes against GPS speed changes, then tells a brake from a start.
  3. Speeding. GPS speed more than 3 km/h over the limit you entered, counted in seconds.
  4. 0–40 test. The phone is still for 1.5 s to fix gravity exactly. The first push over 0.8 m/s² starts the clock, back-dated to where the push began. Speed = ∫ a dt along the forward axis. The clock stops when that speed reaches the target. GPS speed is plotted as a cross-check. Average acceleration = target speed / time.
  • Only test where it's safe and legal, with a passenger holding the phone. Never use the phone while driving.
  • GPS speed updates about once a second and lags by about a second, so the accelerometer times the run.
  • The phone must not slide during the run. Wedge it or use a holder.

How it's measured
  1. A-weighting. Each 4,096-sample block is Fourier-transformed and each frequency is weighted by the A-curve (IEC 61672), which follows how loud people hear it. The weighted energy gives the level in dB(A).
  2. Level. dB = mic level (dBFS) + 115 dB (a typical phone mic, estimated) + your calibration offset.
  3. Leq. The energy average: Leq = 10·log₁₀(mean of 10L/10).
  4. Dose. Allowed time T(L) = 8 h / 2(L − criterion)/exchange. NIOSH: 85 dB(A), 3 dB exchange. India (Factories Act model rules) and US OSHA: 90 dB(A), 5 dB exchange. Dose = Σ (time at L) / T(L). The 8-hour projection is dose × 8 h / time measured.
  • Without calibration, treat numbers as ±5 dB or so. A 3 dB error halves or doubles the allowed time on NIOSH.
  • Very loud impacts (above about 115 dB) can clip the phone mic and read low.
  • A screening tool, not a certified dosimeter.

How it's measured
  1. Tremor. 10 s of all three axes: remove the trend, apply a Hann window, then a Fourier transform. Tremor size is the RMS in 3–20 Hz, from Parseval's theorem. The peak is the strongest frequency in that band.
  2. Reaction. The pad turns green after a random 1.5–4 s. Time runs from the first frame showing green to the finger landing. Taps under 100 ms count as guesses and are retried. The median of 5 is reported.
  3. Verdict. This app's own rule of thumb, not a medical one: tremor at least 1.5× baseline, or reaction at least 40 ms slower, is flagged.
  • About 60 readings a second, so shake up to about 30 Hz can be seen. Hand tremor sits at 8–12 Hz.
  • The touchscreen adds a fixed delay of about 10–30 ms. It's the same in both tests, so the comparison stays fair.
  • Not a medical device.

How it's measured
  1. Impulse. A clap is a short, loud burst of every frequency. What the mic hears afterwards is the room's own echo dying away.
  2. Octave bands. The capture is filtered into octave bands at 250, 500, 1k, 2k and 4k Hz (two band-pass stages each).
  3. Decay curve. Schroeder backward integration (ISO 3382-2): energy left in the room at time t = sum of squared samples from t to the end, with the background noise subtracted and the curve cut where it meets the noise.
  4. RT60 (T20). A straight line is fitted to the curve between −5 and −25 dB, and extrapolated to a 60 dB drop: RT60 = 60 / decay rate. EDT uses 0 to −10 dB. The headline value is the mean of 500 Hz and 1 kHz.
  5. Speech clarity C50. 10·log₁₀(energy in the first 50 ms / energy after), at 1 kHz. Above 0 dB, speech usually comes across clearly.
  6. Limits. ANSI S12.60 classrooms: 0.6 s up to 283 m³, 0.7 s up to 566 m³.
  7. Fix. Sabine: absorption A = 0.161·V / RT. Extra absorption needed = 0.161·V·(1/RTlimit − 1/RTnow) m²; divided by 0.8 (a typical acoustic panel) gives the panel area.
  • A clap gives about 40–50 dB of range above room noise, enough for T20 but not T30. Rows with under 30 dB of range are marked.
  • Claps are weak below 250 Hz, so the bass bands are less certain. A balloon pop works better.
  • Measure with the room in normal use (furniture in, doors shut) and at two or three spots.

How it's measured
  1. Capture. The mic runs at the phone's audio rate (usually 48,000 samples/s). A sudden jump over the background starts a 1.2 s capture.
  2. Main frequency. An 8,192-point Fourier transform (Hann window) of the ringing just after the hit finds the strongest peak from 80 Hz to 12 kHz. The peak is refined between bins.
  3. Decay. The level of that frequency is tracked in 1,024-sample frames (Goertzel filter). A straight line fitted to level (dB) against time gives the decay rate D in dB/s.
  4. Damping. T60 = 60 / D. Damping ratio ζ = D / (8.686 · 2π f). Q = 1 / (2ζ).
  • The mic isn't calibrated, so levels are relative. Frequencies and decay rates don't depend on calibration.
  • Room echo can stretch long ring times. Tap in a quiet spot, close to the mic.
  • Compare like with like: same tap spot, same striker, same support.

How it's measured
  1. Calibrate. For the first 2 s the phone must be still. The average size of the accelerometer reading is your local g.
  2. Vertical acceleration. a = |reading| − g. In a lift almost all the extra acceleration is vertical, so this works whichever way up the phone is.
  3. Speed and height. Integrate a to get speed, then integrate speed to get height. Integration drifts, and a lift cruising at constant speed feels the same as one at rest. So the ride is taken as at rest only at the start and after the doors open, and the speed is corrected in a straight line between those two points.
  4. Jerk. The rate of change of acceleration, from a 0.2 s smoothed signal. Passengers feel jerk as the jolt at the start and end of the run.
  • Walking or swinging the phone adds false acceleration. Keep it still.
  • Height error grows with ride length; expect about ±5–10 % on a few floors.

How it's measured
  1. Swing signal. The gyroscope gives the rotation rate on three axes. The axis that varies most is the swing axis.
  2. Period. Times where the rate crosses zero going up are found between samples. T is the median gap between them.
  3. Large-swing correction. Amplitude θ₀ ≈ peak rate × T / 2π. The small-angle period is T₀ = T / (1 + θ₀²/16).
  4. g. g = 4π² L / T₀².
  5. Damping. The log of each swing's peak rate falls in a straight line. Its slope is the logarithmic decrement δ, and ζ = δ / √(4π² + δ²).
  • A phone isn't a point mass, so L is the distance to its middle. The error is under about 1 % for strings longer than 40 cm.
  • Keep the swing in one plane. Twisting adds a second motion.
